24 core stability, rotational activation, and cardiovascular conditioning exercises that develop the spine stiffness, hip rotation capacity, and golf-specific endurance needed to transfer power efficiently and maintain posture through the full swing.

## What This Category Trains
Core stability exercises develop the anti-rotation and anti-flexion stiffness that allows the lower body to generate force without energy leaking through the lumbar spine. A stiff, stable core is not the same as a mobile one — these exercises train the body to resist unwanted movement while the hips and thorax produce rotation. Anti-rotation pressing (Pallof press variants, cable chops) is the foundational pattern. Rotational activation exercises address hip internal rotation capacity — the primary driver of pelvic clearing in the downswing. Cardiovascular conditioning exercises develop the golf-specific aerobic and anaerobic capacity to sustain swing mechanics through 18 holes and training sessions.

## TPI Screens Addressed
Core and rotational exercises target restrictions identified in:
- [[torso-rotation]] — cable and band chop/lift patterns load thoracic rotation through full range under axial load
- [[seated-trunk-rotation]] — Torso Rotation and Pallof Press variants reinforce thoracic mobility from a stable pelvis
- [[pelvic-rotation]] — hip internal rotation exercises directly address pelvis-to-femur rotation deficit
- [[lower-quarter-rotation]] — KB and MB hip rotation exercises target lower limb rotational range
- [[overhead-deep-squat]] — hip internal rotation capacity affects squat mechanics and impacts screen performance
- [[toe-touch]] — hip rotation patterns loaded in hip flexion influence posterior chain length indirectly

## Golf Application
Anti-rotation core strength prevents early extension, reverse spine, and C-posture by keeping the lumbar spine stable while the hips and thorax rotate independently. Pallof press and cable chop variants are the most direct training stimulus for this quality, supporting [[backswing-stay-centered]] and [[address-alignment]]. Hip internal rotation activation (KB Goblet Standing Hip Internal Rotation, MB Bear Hug Standing Hip Internal Rotation) directly addresses the pelvic clearing sequence in the downswing — when the pelvis cannot internally rotate on the lead side, the golfer is forced to slide rather than rotate, limiting speed and creating early extension (see [[impact-more-open-at-impact]] and [[downswing-get-to-lead-side]]). Lateral agility and speed ladder conditioning build the lower body reactivity that underpins explosive rotation and weight transfer under fatigue.

## Programming Notes
**Activation block placement:** Anti-rotation press and chop/lift activation exercises belong in the warm-up block before strength work (2–3 sets × 8–12 reps). They prime core stiffness and thoracic rotation before the body is loaded. Hip internal rotation exercises (KB Goblet, MB Bear Hug) are highest priority when [[pelvic-rotation]] or [[lower-quarter-rotation]] screen failures are documented.

**Main session core block:** Programme Bird Dog, Dead Bug, Bicycle Crunch, Bodyweight Hang, and DB Side Bend as a dedicated core block between the strength main block and the power block (2–4 sets × 8–15 reps). These exercises develop anti-flexion and anti-lateral flexion capacity that is missed by rotational work alone.

**Anti-rotation progressions:** Entry point is tall kneeling (1/2 Kneeling Cable Anti-Rotation Press, 1/2 Kneeling Plate Chop & Lift). Progress to split stance (RB Split Stance Torso Rotation) and then to standing neutral stance (Cable/RB Pallof Press + Rotation) as stability improves. The standing + rotation variant has the highest transfer to the golf swing.

**Rotational power:** 3D Strap Torso Rotations and Cable Chop/Lift patterns at controlled tempo develop rotational motor control. For power expression, escalate tempo and progress to the medicine ball rotational exercises listed in [[upper-body-strength-exercises]].

**Conditioning block:** Lateral Shuffle, Lateral Skipping, and Speed Ladders are warm-up or finisher conditioning tools (3–5 sets × 10–20 m or 30–45 s). They develop the lower body reactivity and lateral agility that underpins dynamic weight transfer and recovery footwork.
